# Grammarly AI in 2026: Still Worth Paying For, or Has Claude Killed It?

I've been using Grammarly for nine years. When I cancelled my Premium subscription last June after testing [Claude](/claude-ai-2026-complete-guide) and ChatGPT as writing partners for a month, I thought I was done with it. I re-subscribed in September. Not because the LLMs got worse, but because Grammarly does something they can't: it edits my writing *where I'm writing it*, at the moment I'm writing it.

That distinction matters more than the AI tool comparison articles usually admit.

## The scenario that changed my mind

In July, I was drafting a contract renewal proposal for a managed services client. Roughly $180K annual value, due by end of week. I had Claude open in one browser tab and the proposal in Word on my second monitor.

Here's what actually happened: I'd write a paragraph, tab over to Claude, paste it, ask for tone adjustments, copy the response, paste it back into Word, reformat, continue. Each round-trip took 45-90 seconds. For a 2,400-word proposal, that's painful.

With Grammarly running in Word, the suggestions appear inline. Hover, accept or reject, move on. No context switching. I timed it on a similar proposal in October — same length, same complexity. Grammarly Premium: 2 hours 15 minutes. The LLM round-trip workflow from July: 3 hours 40 minutes.

That's not nothing. That's more than an hour of billable time per proposal.

## What Grammarly AI actually is in 2026

Grammarly isn't one product anymore. It's three layered features stacked on the same core engine.

**The grammar and style engine** is the original product. Spelling, grammar, clarity suggestions, tone detection. It runs in every app you can install it in — browsers, Microsoft Word, Google Docs, Outlook, Slack, mobile keyboards. This is the part that's been iterating since 2009, and it's frankly the best in the category at what it does.

**GrammarlyGO** is the generative AI layer added in 2023. It rewrites sentences, shifts tone, expands or compresses prose, generates drafts from prompts. It's less impressive than Claude or GPT-5 for long-form writing. It's more useful than either of them for in-context editing because it appears where your text already exists.

**Grammarly for Business** adds brand voice training, security controls, and usage analytics for teams. This is the tier that justifies the price jump from $12 to $15/user/month. If you're a solo writer, you won't use most of it. If you're running a marketing team where tone consistency across writers matters, it earns its keep.

If you're evaluating writing tools broadly, the [best AI tools in 2026 guide](/best-ai-tools-2026) covers how Grammarly stacks up against Jasper, Claude, and the rest.

## Pricing — and why the free tier is genuinely useful

**Free:** Basic grammar, spelling, punctuation, and clarity suggestions. Covers ~60% of what most people need.

**Premium:** $12/month billed annually ($30/month monthly). Full style, tone, clarity, and GrammarlyGO access.

**Business:** $15/user/month. Brand voice, admin controls, security controls.

**Enterprise:** Custom pricing.

The free tier is the unusual part. Most AI tools gate everything worthwhile behind a paywall. Grammarly's free tier genuinely works for students, casual email writers, and anyone who writes a few hundred words a day. I recommend it to friends who ask me what to install on their laptop before job applications.

Premium is where the math starts getting interesting. $12/month against the time savings I described above pays for itself in the first 90 minutes of usage in any given month.

## Where it loses to Claude and ChatGPT

Let's not pretend Grammarly wins everywhere. The generative side is genuinely weaker than the top LLMs.

I tested GrammarlyGO against Claude Sonnet 4.6 on a 3,000-word quarterly report last month. Same source material, same target audience, same tone brief. Claude produced something I'd send to a client with minor edits. GrammarlyGO produced something that read cleanly but felt generic — like it smoothed the edges off the actual argument.

Long-form structure, complex reasoning, multi-document synthesis — use Claude or GPT-5 for those. I keep [Claude Pro](/claude-ai-2026-complete-guide) running for anything over 1,500 words or anything requiring genuine analysis.

The other loss: Grammarly can't think about what you should be writing. It can only react to what you've already written. When I'm genuinely stuck on how to frame an argument, Grammarly has nothing useful to say. Claude does.

## The integration story is the actual moat

Here's what I think most reviews miss about Grammarly. It isn't competing on model quality — it stopped being able to do that around 2023. It's competing on surface area.

Grammarly works in:
- Every browser (Chrome, Safari, Firefox, Edge)
- Microsoft Word (desktop and web)
- Google Docs
- Outlook
- Slack
- Apple Mail
- LinkedIn messaging
- The iOS and Android keyboards

Try running Claude inline in LinkedIn messaging. You can't. Try running Claude inside Slack without switching apps. You can, with the Anthropic app, but the experience is nothing like having AI appear as you type. Grammarly is ambient. The others are destinations.

This is why I came back. I can explain it concisely: the switching cost of tab-hopping between my document and an LLM chat interface, multiplied across every email and document I write in a week, is higher than $12/month.

## What Grammarly still gets wrong

Two things that genuinely annoy me and haven't been fixed.

First, **tone detection overreacts to casual writing**. If you write "hey, got a minute?" to a colleague on Slack, Grammarly flags it as informal as though you should have said "Dear John, do you have time available?" The machine learning understands formal tone shifts but hasn't learned which apps need formality and which don't.

Second, **the Business tier's brand voice training is not as smart as the marketing suggests**. I set one up for a client in March. We fed it 40 approved pieces of marketing copy. The output for the next 60 days mimicked sentence structure but consistently missed the brand's two signature rhetorical moves (a dry opener, a concrete data point in paragraph two). If brand voice is critical, you still need a human editor. The AI gets 70% of the way there.

## What I'd do differently if I were starting over

I'd skip Grammarly entirely during the research and drafting phase. Use Claude or GPT-5 for the actual composition — they're better at generating ideas and structuring arguments. Turn Grammarly on during the editing and polishing phase, especially if the final document will live in Word, Google Docs, or email.

I'd also skip Premium if I only write casually. The free tier plus an occasional paste into Claude is a reasonable workflow for anyone writing less than 2,000 words a week.

For teams, I'd only pay for Business tier if tone consistency across multiple writers actively matters — for example, if clients have flagged inconsistent voice before. For most internal teams, Premium is fine.

If you're trying to decide between writing-focused AI tools, it's worth comparing against Jasper AI's more aggressive marketing-team positioning and Notion AI's workspace-native approach. They serve different workflows than Grammarly does.

## The verdict

Grammarly in 2026 is not the best AI at any one thing. It's the most present AI. That's a different competitive position than Claude or ChatGPT, and it's not going away — unless one of the big LLMs builds equivalent surface-area integration, which none of them seem interested in doing.

At $12/month, Premium is a calculator decision. If you write for a living, it pays for itself. If you're a student, the free tier is enough. If you're a team of writers managing brand voice, Business is worth testing for 90 days before committing.

I'm not re-cancelling again.
